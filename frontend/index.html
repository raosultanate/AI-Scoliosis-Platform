The traditional clinical method

How a radiologist measures a Cobb angle

The same method doctors have used with a ruler and protractor since 1948. SpineAngle's calculation is built to match it exactly — just done with trigonometry instead of a pencil.

  1. 1

    Find the two "end vertebrae"

    Looking down the spine, the radiologist finds the vertebra above the curve that is tilted most steeply into it, and the one below that is tilted most steeply the other way. These bookends are called the upper and lower end vertebrae — everything between them is the curve being measured.

  2. 2

    Trace the tilted edges

    A line is drawn along the top edge of the upper end vertebra, and another along the bottom edge of the lower end vertebra. In medical terms, these edges are called endplates.

  3. 3

    Extend the lines until they cross

    The two endplates usually tilt toward each other, so extending both lines far enough makes them meet at a point, often well off to one side of the image. (Some radiologists instead draw a perpendicular off each endplate and measure where those cross — easier to fit on a small film, and mathematically the exact same angle. See why below.)

  4. 4

    Measure the angle between them

    The angle at that crossing point is the Cobb angle — traditionally read off with a protractor, or with a digital angle tool on modern scanning software. A bigger angle means a sharper curve.

The math, plainly

Every line has a "tilt" — how many degrees it leans away from perfectly flat. tilt = arctan( rise ÷ run ) rise = how far the line moves up or down between its two ends run = how far it moves side to side Cobb angle = the difference between the two endplates' tilts, corrected to always land between 0° and 90°

Why perpendiculars give the same answer: rotating two lines by the same 90° never changes the angle between them — like turning two crossed rulers together. So a radiologist can measure whichever pair of lines is easier to draw.

The computer's version of the same idea

How SpineAngle's AI will evaluate it

This splits into two stages. Measuring — turning known vertebra corners into an angle — is real and running in this preview today. Seeing — finding those corners automatically on a real X-ray — is a planned next step, not yet connected. That's why this preview offers a safe worked example instead of accepting a real X-ray for analysis.

Live in this preview

Stage 1 — Measuring

Every vertebra is described by four corner points (top-left, top-right, bottom-left, bottom-right) — currently supplied by a clinician's own annotation of the image, the same starting point a radiologist works from.

  • For every vertebra, the computer builds a line for its top edge and a line for its bottom edge from those four corners.
  • It calculates each line's exact tilt using the same trigonometry a radiologist's protractor approximates by hand — just computed precisely instead of read off a scale.
  • Rather than checking only the vertebrae a human eye would pick, it tests the angle for every possible pair of "upper vertebra top edge" and "lower vertebra bottom edge," in top-to-bottom order, and keeps the largest one — the same "most-tilted pair" idea a radiologist uses, just verified exhaustively instead of by eye.
  • Every number behind the final angle — which vertebrae were used, their exact tilt — is shown alongside the result, so the answer can be checked, not just trusted.
Planned next step

Stage 2 — Seeing

Right now, a person has to mark those four corners per vertebra by hand. The planned next step trains a vision model on many X-rays, each labeled by a clinician, so it learns to recognize a vertebra's corners the way a trained eye does.

Once that model hands off the same four corner points, Stage 1's math runs completely unchanged. Teaching the computer to see a vertebra doesn't change how the angle itself gets calculated — this project deliberately keeps those two jobs separate, so a smarter corner-finder later never touches the measuring logic already verified today.

The math, plainly

For each endplate line, from its two corner points (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂): tilt = atan2( y₂−y₁, x₂−x₁ ) — adjusted to always fall between −90° and 90°, since a line can't tell "up" from "down" along itself For each candidate pair of endplates: angle = the smaller of |tilt₁ − tilt₂| and 180° − |tilt₁ − tilt₂| Cobb angle = the largest angle found across every valid pair (always between 0° and 90°)

This is the exact "difference in tilt" idea used by hand — just checked exactly, for every vertebra, in a fraction of a second, with the working shown.
